Most growth plans assume the people will be there. A new location, a new service line, a new venture. The plan covers the market, the budget, and the timeline. It rarely names who is going to run it.

When the time comes, the answer is usually one of two things. The founder takes it on personally, on top of everything else. Or the company rushes an outside hire into a role nobody has had time to define.

Both can work. Neither is a strategy. The alternative is slower and less exciting: build a bench of people inside the business who are ready, or nearly ready, to take on more before the opportunity arrives.

## The bench is a capacity constraint

In a portfolio, I have come to think the real limit on growth is rarely capital or ideas. It is the number of people who can be trusted to run something.

Money can be raised or saved. Ideas are everywhere. A person who understands how the business works, shares its values, and can make good decisions without supervision takes years to develop. When you need one and do not have one, there is no quick way to get one.

That makes leadership development a capital allocation question in its own right. Time and money spent developing people is an investment in future capacity, and like any investment, it has to be made before the return is needed.

## Who belongs on it

A bench is not a list of everyone who might someday be promoted. It is a short list of people who show specific signs of being able to carry more.

The signs I look for are practical:

- **They own outcomes, not tasks.** When something goes wrong in their area, they fix it without being asked and tell you what happened.
- **They make other people better.** Colleagues seek them out, and the people around them improve.
- **They think beyond their role.** They ask questions about how the business makes money, why decisions were made, and what is coming next.
- **They handle pressure with steadiness.** Not without stress, but without passing it on to everyone else.
- **Their judgment matches the company's values.** When they face a choice nobody is watching, they make the one you would want them to make.

Notice that none of these is about technical skill. Technical skill matters, but it is the easiest part to teach.

## How to develop them

The most effective development is real responsibility, given in increasing amounts, with support.

**Give them a piece of something real.** A project, a client relationship, a process to fix, a small team to lead. Something where the outcome matters and they own it.

**Expose them to how decisions get made.** Bring them into planning conversations, budget discussions, and reviews they would not normally see. Explain the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

**Let them make recoverable mistakes.** The urge to step in is strong. Resist it when the cost of the mistake is small and the lesson is valuable.

**Give them honest feedback, often.** Not once a year in a formal review. Regularly, specifically, and with the expectation that they will act on it.

**Pair them with someone experienced.** Time with a leader who has already done the job is often worth more than any formal training.

## The founder has to let go

The biggest obstacle to building a bench is usually the founder.

Developing people means handing them work the founder could do faster and, at first, better. It means watching decisions get made differently than you would make them. It means accepting a period where things are a little slower and a little messier while someone learns.

Founders who cannot tolerate that end up with a team that waits for instructions, and then wonder why nobody is ready to step up. I wrote about a related version of this in [Hiring your first real operator](https://t47group.com/blog/hiring-your-first-real-operator/): the hardest part is not finding the person, it is actually giving them the job.

## Be honest with the people on it

Developing someone creates expectations. If a person is being prepared for more responsibility, they will reasonably expect it to come.

That requires honesty on both sides. Be clear about what you see in them, what they need to work on, and what kind of opportunity might realistically open up. Do not promise a specific role on a specific timeline unless you can deliver it.

It also means accepting that some of the people you develop will leave for opportunities you cannot offer. That is a real cost. It is still better than a company where nobody is worth recruiting.

## Start with one name

The exercise does not need to be elaborate. Look at each part of the business and ask a simple question: if the person running this left, or if we doubled it, who would step in?

If the answer is nobody, that is the first development priority. Pick one person, give them more responsibility this quarter, and pay attention to how they handle it.

A bench is built one person at a time, over years. The best time to start is before the plan needs it.
