December planning has a predictable shape. Leadership teams gather, review the year, and write down what they want to accomplish next year. New products, new markets, new hires, new systems.

What almost never gets written down is what they are going to stop doing.

That is the problem. Every new priority needs time, attention, and money, and none of those appear out of nowhere. If nothing is removed, the new goals get piled on top of everything the team was already doing, and by spring most of them have quietly stalled.

Before setting goals for 2027, I think every owner should write a stop-doing list.

## Why stopping is so hard

Businesses accumulate work the way houses accumulate things. Each item came in for a reason. A report someone once asked for. A meeting that solved a problem two years ago. A service offered to one customer that became a standing commitment. A product line that never really took off but never quite failed.

Nobody decides to keep these things. They just stay, because stopping requires a decision and continuing does not.

There is also an emotional cost. Stopping something can feel like admitting it was a mistake, or like letting down the person who started it. So it is easier to let it run.

The result is a business carrying a steady load of work that no longer earns its place.

## Where to look

A useful stop-doing list usually draws from a few categories.

**Meetings.** List every recurring meeting and ask what decision or action it produces. If the answer is unclear, it is a candidate.

**Reports.** Which reports are produced regularly, and who actually reads them? Ask the people who receive them. The answers are often surprising.

**Products, services, and customers.** Which offerings take a disproportionate amount of effort for what they return? Are there customers who cost more to serve than they are worth?

**Tools and subscriptions.** The [bottom-up budget](https://t47group.com/blog/build-the-budget-from-zero/) exercise is a good place to catch these. Anything nobody would sign up for again today should be questioned.

**Initiatives.** Projects that were started with energy and have since drifted. If a project has not made meaningful progress in two quarters, decide whether to recommit or stop.

**The owner's own work.** This is the hardest category and the most important. What is the owner still doing that someone else should own? Often it is the work that feels most comfortable, which is exactly why it stays.

## How to decide

For each candidate, ask a few simple questions:

1. If we were not already doing this, would we start it today?
2. What would actually happen if we stopped?
3. Who would notice, and how much would they care?
4. What could we do with the time and money it frees up?

The first question does most of the work. A surprising amount of what businesses do would never be started from scratch.

For the second question, it can help to test rather than guess. Pause a report or a meeting for a month and see whether anyone asks for it. If nobody does, you have your answer.

## Make the trade explicit

The point of the list is not just to stop things. It is to fund the new priorities with the capacity that frees up.

When the leadership team sets a goal for next year, it should be paired with what is being removed to make room for it. A new product line might be funded by ending an underperforming service. A major hiring effort might be paired with cutting two recurring meetings and a report. The trade makes the cost of the new goal visible, and it forces a conversation about whether the goal is worth it.

That is the same logic behind [knowing when to kill a venture](https://t47group.com/blog/when-to-kill-a-venture/), applied at a smaller scale. Capacity is finite. Choosing what to stop is part of choosing what to build.

## Communicate it clearly

Stopping things affects people, and how it is communicated matters.

- Explain why the decision was made, not just what is changing.
- Thank the people who built or ran what is ending. Their effort was real even if the thing no longer fits.
- Tell customers early and directly if a change affects them, and help them with the transition where you can.
- Show where the freed-up capacity is going, so people can see that stopping one thing is making room for something better.

## Start small, but start

A stop-doing list does not need to be long. Three meaningful items will free up more capacity than most teams expect.

The end of the year is the right moment. The calendar is about to reset, budgets are being set, and people are already thinking about what next year should look like. Starting January without the old commitments is far easier than trying to drop them in the middle of the year.

Write the goals for 2027. Just write the stop-doing list first. It is usually the shorter document, and it is often the one that decides whether the goals get done.
