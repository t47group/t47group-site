Most of the attention in a small business acquisition goes to the deal. The valuation, the diligence, the financing, the closing documents. By the time everything is signed, the buyer is exhausted and relieved.

Then the actual work begins. The first few months after closing decide whether the business you bought becomes the business you hoped for, and they are where a surprising number of acquisitions go wrong.

I wrote earlier about [what I look for before buying a small business](https://t47group.com/blog/what-i-look-for-before-buying-a-small-business/). This is the other half: what to pay attention to once it is yours.

## Change less than you want to

Every new owner arrives with a list of improvements. Some came from diligence. Some came from experience running other businesses. Most of them are probably right.

Almost none of them should happen in the first month.

The people who work in the business are watching closely. They want to know whether the new owner respects what they built, whether their jobs are safe, and whether the place they have worked for years is about to become unrecognizable. Sweeping changes in the first weeks answer those questions in the worst possible way, even when the changes are good ones.

There are exceptions. If something is unsafe, illegal, or actively losing money, fix it immediately. Everything else can wait until you understand the business well enough to know which of your ideas actually fit.

## Learn the business from the inside

The first priority is learning how the business really works, which is never quite how it looked from the outside.

Spend time where the work happens. Sit with the people who answer the phones, schedule the work, send the invoices, and deal with customers. Ask them what works, what frustrates them, and what they would change if they could. Then mostly listen.

Some of the most valuable knowledge in a small business is undocumented. It lives with long-tenured employees who know which customers need special handling, which vendors deliver, and why certain processes exist. That knowledge is part of what you paid for, and the only way to get it is to earn enough trust that people share it.

## Protect the key people

In diligence, you identified the people the business depends on. After closing, keeping them is one of the most important jobs you have.

Meet with each of them early and individually. Tell them plainly that they matter, what you see as their role going forward, and what you expect from them. Uncertainty is what drives good people to leave, and a new owner creates a great deal of it.

Where it makes sense, give them reasons to stay that go beyond words: a clear role, real authority, and, if appropriate, compensation or incentives tied to the business's future. The capable second-in-command who has been quietly running more than anyone realized is often worth more than any line on the balance sheet.

## Stay close to customers

Customers notice ownership changes too, and some will wonder whether the service they rely on is about to change.

Reach out to the largest and longest-standing ones early, ideally with the seller or a trusted employee alongside you if the relationship allows it. The message is simple: we bought this business because of how well it serves you, and we intend to keep it that way.

Then make sure nothing slips. Missed deliveries, billing errors, or slower responses in the first few months are exactly what customers will attribute to the new owner, fairly or not.

## Use the seller well

If the seller has agreed to stay on for a transition period, use that time deliberately.

Have them introduce you to key customers, vendors, and partners. Ask them to walk you through the decisions they make that nobody else does. Write down what they tell you. Transition periods tend to feel long at the start and short at the end.

It also helps to agree early on how the seller's role will wind down. Ambiguity about who is really in charge confuses employees and customers alike.

## Get the financial controls in place

While operations stay largely unchanged, the financial side usually needs attention early.

Make sure you have clear visibility into cash, receivables, and payables from day one. Understand who can spend money, approve payments, and sign contracts, and adjust that where needed. Separate the business's finances cleanly from anything that belonged to the previous owner personally.

This is also the time to start the [weekly cash habit](https://t47group.com/blog/cash-discipline-is-a-weekly-habit/) if the business does not already have one. A new owner who does not know the cash position is managing blind.

## Plan the changes, then make them in order

Somewhere around the end of the first quarter, you should know the business well enough to decide what to change.

Prioritize ruthlessly. Pick the few changes that matter most, explain the reasoning to the team, and make them one at a time. Involve the people closest to the work in how the changes are made. They will see problems you cannot, and they are far more likely to support a change they helped shape.

Then measure. If a change does not produce the result you expected, be willing to adjust or reverse it. Admitting a mistake early builds more credibility with a new team than pushing forward to save face.

## The real goal

The point of the first hundred days is not to put your stamp on the business. It is to earn the trust of the people who make it work, so that the improvements you make later actually stick.

A business that was worth buying was doing something right before you arrived. The first job of a new owner is to find out exactly what that was and protect it. Everything else comes after.
