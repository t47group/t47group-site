Running one company, capital allocation mostly happens by default. Cash comes in, bills go out, and whatever is left gets spent on whatever seems most pressing.

Running several companies makes that impossible. Every dollar of free cash could go in a handful of directions, and each company has a reasonable case for why it should be theirs. Without a process, the loudest request wins, or the founder's current favorite does.

We try to treat each dollar as a decision. These are the options, and how I think about them.

## The five places a dollar can go

Every dollar of free cash in a portfolio ends up in one of a few places. It can be reinvested in the company that produced it. It can be moved to another company in the portfolio. It can fund something new. It can pay down debt. Or it can be held as reserve.

Taking a distribution is a sixth option, and an important one. But for a holding company that is still building, most of the real choices are among the first five.

Naming them sounds trivial. It is useful because it forces a comparison. A request to reinvest is no longer judged on whether it is a good idea. It is judged on whether it is a better idea than the alternatives.

## Reinvestment in the business that earned it

The default should be reinvestment in the company that produced the cash, as long as there is a good use for it.

That company understands its own market. It has a team in place, customers who trust it, and a track record you can evaluate. A dollar spent improving something that already works often returns more than a dollar spent on something that does not exist yet.

The test is whether the reinvestment produces a return the business could not get otherwise. Hiring ahead of known demand usually qualifies. Expanding into a market adjacent to the one you already serve usually qualifies. Spending because the budget allows it does not.

## Moving cash between companies

This is the defining feature of a portfolio and the place it can go most wrong.

Done well, it means a stable, cash-producing business funds a younger one through a period it could not survive alone. That is how [Xsatori Labs](https://xsatorilabs.net) and the products built around it have been funded without outside investors.

Done poorly, it means a healthy business is slowly starved to keep a struggling one alive. The mature company stops getting the investment it needs, its competitive position erodes, and the portfolio ends up with two weak companies instead of one strong one.

I want every transfer between companies to have a purpose, an expected timeline, and a point at which we review whether it is working.

## New ventures

New ideas always look attractive, because they have not yet met reality. That is precisely why they should face the highest bar.

We fund new ventures in stages, with small amounts that answer specific questions. Does anyone want this? Will they pay for it? Can we build it at a cost that makes sense? Only when those questions have encouraging answers does the next amount get released.

This feels slow, and sometimes it is. But it means the portfolio is never betting heavily on something that has not earned it.

## Debt and reserves

Paying down debt is a guaranteed return equal to the interest rate. That makes it a useful benchmark. If a proposed investment cannot clearly beat the return of simply reducing debt, it should probably wait.

Reserves are harder to value because their return only shows up when something goes wrong. But every operator who has been through a downturn knows what cash on hand is worth when everyone else is scrambling. I would rather have a reserve that feels excessive in good years than discover it was too small in a bad one.

## The discipline that holds it together

The process matters more than any individual decision. We review allocation on a regular schedule, not when someone asks. Every company makes its case in the same format. And we look back at past allocations to see which ones worked, so the next round is informed by evidence rather than optimism.

Most allocation mistakes are not dramatic. They are small, repeated, and comfortable: continuing to fund something because it was funded last time. A regular, structured review is the cheapest protection against that.

It is not glamorous. It is the work that decides what the portfolio looks like in ten years.
