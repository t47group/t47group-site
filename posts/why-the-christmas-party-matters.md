Every year at [FLAT](https://flatstaffing.com) we have a Christmas party. We run holiday contests at Halloween and Thanksgiving. We do team-building and we volunteer together.

None of this is unusual on its own. What sometimes surprises people is how seriously we take it, in a business where margins are watched closely and where it would be easy to treat these things as optional.

I think they are some of the most practical things we do.

## Staffing is a people business in both directions

A staffing company serves clients, but the product is people. Recruiters who answer the phone at six in the morning when a shift needs to be filled. Coordinators who know which associate is reliable on a forklift and which one needs another week of training. Account managers who hear about a client's problem before the client has fully described it.

That work is fast, repetitive, and often stressful. It depends on judgment that does not show up in a system. And it is very easy for people doing it to start feeling like they are just part of the machinery.

Traditions push against that. They are a regular, visible reminder that the people doing the work matter as people, not only as capacity.

## Small rituals do more than big programs

I have seen companies spend real money on culture initiatives that nobody remembers a year later. A new set of values on the wall. An offsite with an agenda. A program that launches with energy and fades by spring.

What tends to last is smaller and more repetitive. The same party every year, so people look forward to it and new hires hear about it before they experience it. A costume contest that people talk about for a week. A Thanksgiving contest that gets a little competitive between teams.

These things work because they recur. Culture is mostly what happens repeatedly, and an annual tradition is one of the few things that reliably does.

## It gives managers a different kind of conversation

Most of what a manager says to their team is about work. That is appropriate. It also means that, over months, a relationship can narrow to schedules, problems, and deadlines.

A party or a contest is a low-pressure setting where people talk about other things. Families, plans, what they did last weekend. That sounds minor. It is often where a manager learns that someone is under strain at home, or is quietly looking for more responsibility, or has a skill nobody knew about.

Those conversations make the working ones go better for the rest of the year.

## Volunteering changes the frame

Volunteering together does something a party cannot. It puts the team in a setting where nobody is the boss in the usual sense, and where the work is for someone else entirely.

People who spend their week solving problems for clients get to spend a few hours on something where the only question is whether it helped. It also reminds everyone that a business is part of a community, not just a set of transactions within one. Phoenix has been good to us. It seems right to give some of that back where we can.

## It is not a substitute for the fundamentals

I want to be clear about the limits. A good party does not fix poor pay, unclear expectations, or a bad manager. If those problems exist, a holiday event can feel hollow or even insulting.

Traditions work when the basics are already in place. People are paid fairly. They know what is expected of them. They are treated with respect in ordinary weeks, not only in December. When that is true, the traditions add something. When it is not, fix that first.

## The cost is small and the signal is large

Compared with the cost of turnover, the cost of a party and a few contests is modest. Replacing an experienced recruiter or coordinator takes months and affects every client they supported.

I do not claim that a Christmas party keeps people in their jobs. Many things go into that decision. But I have come to believe that people stay where they feel seen, and that being seen is built from a lot of small moments, a few of which happen to involve costumes and a long table of food.

That is reason enough to keep doing it, and to keep doing it well.
