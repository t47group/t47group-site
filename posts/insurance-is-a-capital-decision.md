Most owners treat insurance as a bill. It arrives once a year, it goes up more often than it goes down, and the main question is whether the broker can find something cheaper.

I think that framing misses what insurance actually is. Every policy is a decision about which risks the business will carry with its own capital and which it will pay someone else to carry. That is a capital allocation question, and it deserves the same attention as any other.

## Every risk has three options

For any meaningful risk a business faces, there are really only three choices.

**Avoid it.** Do not take the job, enter the market, or offer the service that creates the exposure.

**Carry it.** Accept the risk and hold enough reserve to absorb the loss if it happens.

**Transfer it.** Pay an insurer to take on some or all of the loss.

Insurance is the third option. It makes sense when the potential loss is large enough to damage the business and unlikely enough that paying a premium is cheaper than holding a reserve big enough to cover it.

It makes less sense for small, frequent, predictable losses. Insuring against something that happens every year usually means paying the insurer to process losses you could have simply budgeted for, plus their overhead.

## Start from what could actually hurt you

The useful way to review coverage is to start from the risks, not the policies.

Sit down with your leadership team and list the events that could seriously damage the business. A major injury on a job. A liability claim from a client. A data breach. A fire or flood. The loss of a key person. A large customer failing to pay. A vehicle accident involving an employee.

For each one, ask three questions:

1. How likely is it, honestly?
2. How much would it cost if it happened?
3. Could the business survive that cost from its own reserves?

The events that are unlikely but would be devastating are the core of your insurance program. The events that are likely but manageable may be better handled by reserves, deductibles, or better operations.

## Deductibles are a capital decision too

A higher deductible lowers the premium because the business is agreeing to carry more of each loss itself.

Whether that makes sense depends on the business's reserves and how often claims happen. A company with healthy cash reserves and few claims can often save by raising deductibles and holding the difference. A company with thin reserves may need lower deductibles even at a higher cost, because a single claim could create a cash problem.

The point is to choose on purpose. Most businesses accept whatever deductible was in place last year without asking whether it still fits.

## Questions to ask at renewal

Renewal season is the natural time to review. A few questions worth bringing to your broker:

- **What has changed in our business this year?** New services, locations, vehicles, employees, or types of clients can all change exposure. Coverage built for last year's business may not fit this year's.
- **What does this policy exclude?** The exclusions often matter more than the coverage. Understand specifically what is not covered.
- **Are our limits still right?** Limits set years ago may not reflect the size of the business or the size of the contracts it now signs.
- **What do our client contracts require?** Many contracts specify insurance types and limits. Make sure coverage matches what you have agreed to.
- **Where are the gaps between policies?** Some losses fall between general liability, professional liability, cyber, and other coverages. Ask directly where those gaps are.
- **How have our claims affected our pricing?** Understanding what drives your premium helps you decide where better safety or operations could pay off.

This is general guidance, not insurance or legal advice. A licensed broker who understands your industry should review your actual coverage.

## Operations are the cheapest insurance

The best way to lower the cost of risk is to reduce the risk itself.

Safety programs, training, clear procedures, good contracts, and careful hiring all lower the likelihood of claims. Over time, that tends to show up in better pricing and fewer disruptions. It also protects people, which matters more than any premium.

In labor businesses especially, safety is not a side topic. It is one of the most important things the company does, both for the people doing the work and for the long-term cost of operating.

## Review it alongside everything else

In a portfolio, insurance is one of the areas where the center should stay involved. A serious uninsured loss in one company can reach the others, and decisions about structure and coverage are among the few that should not be left entirely to each business.

But the larger lesson applies to any company. Treat insurance as part of how you allocate capital. Decide which risks you will carry and which you will transfer, size the reserves that go with that decision, and revisit it every year.

The premium is not the cost of insurance. The cost is what happens if you got the decision wrong.
