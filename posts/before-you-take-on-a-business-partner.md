Most business partnerships start with enthusiasm and a handshake. Two people trust each other, share an idea, and want to get moving. Paperwork feels like a formality, and hard questions feel like a sign of distrust.

That is backwards. The best time to settle the difficult questions is at the beginning, when everyone is optimistic and nobody has anything to protect. Once money, time, and ego are invested, the same conversation becomes much harder.

Partnerships that come apart rarely fail because of the idea. They fail because of something the partners never talked about.

## Who does what

The first conversation is about roles, and it needs to be specific.

"We will both work on everything" is not a division of labor. It is a recipe for duplicated effort in some places and gaps in others. Decide who owns sales, operations, finance, product, and hiring. Decide who has the final say in each area when you disagree.

Then talk about time. Is everyone full-time? If one partner is keeping another job or running another business, say so, and agree on what that means for expectations. Mismatched effort is one of the most common sources of resentment, and it almost always starts with assumptions nobody said out loud.

## How decisions get made

Day-to-day decisions should belong to whoever owns that area. But some decisions are big enough that they need everyone.

Write down which ones. Taking on debt, bringing in new partners or investors, selling major assets, hiring senior people, changing the direction of the business. Agree on how those decisions get made and what happens when you cannot agree.

A deadlock between two equal partners can freeze a company. It is worth deciding in advance how a tie gets broken, whether that is a trusted advisor, a defined process, or one partner holding a deciding vote in specific areas.

## Money in, money out

Money conversations are uncomfortable, which is exactly why they need to happen early.

- **Contributions.** Who is putting in cash, time, equipment, or relationships, and how is each valued?
- **Future capital.** If the business needs more money, who provides it, and what happens to ownership if one partner can contribute and the other cannot?
- **Compensation.** Who gets paid a salary, how much, and when? A partner working full-time and a partner contributing only capital often have very different views on this.
- **Distributions.** When profits exist, how much stays in the business and how much goes to the owners?

There is no single right answer to any of these. There is only the answer you agreed on in advance and the one you argue about later.

## Vesting and commitment

Ownership that is granted all at once, on day one, assumes everyone will stay committed for the long haul. Sometimes that is true. Sometimes a partner leaves after six months and still owns half the company.

Vesting ownership over time, so it is earned by continued involvement, protects everyone, including the partner who stays. It is common in startups and worth considering in any partnership where the business depends on what each person contributes over time.

## How it ends

This is the conversation people most want to skip, and the one that matters most.

Every partnership ends eventually. Someone wants to retire, move on, or start something else. Someone gets sick or dies. Someone wants to sell and the other does not. The relationship breaks down.

A good partnership agreement answers in advance:

1. Can a partner sell their share, and to whom?
2. Does the company or the other partner have the right to buy it first?
3. How is the business valued when that happens?
4. How is the buyout paid, and over what period?
5. What happens if a partner dies or becomes unable to work?
6. What happens if a partner leaves to compete?

These are the core of what attorneys call a buy-sell agreement, and in many cases it is worth having one drafted specifically for your situation.

## Put it in writing, with help

Everything here should end up in a written agreement, reviewed by an attorney. That might be an operating agreement, a partnership agreement, or a shareholder agreement, depending on how the business is structured.

This is general advice, not legal advice. The right structure depends on your state, your entity type, and your circumstances. Paying for good legal work at the start is almost always cheaper than paying for a dispute later.

## The real test

The conversations themselves tell you something. If you and a prospective partner cannot discuss money, control, and exits calmly now, when nothing is at stake, it will not get easier when something is.

A partner who welcomes those conversations is usually one worth having. One who treats them as an insult is giving you information you should take seriously.

Good partnerships are built on trust. Trust is easier to keep when everyone already knows what was agreed.
