There are two common attitudes toward business debt, and both are wrong.

The first says debt is dangerous and should be avoided at all costs. The second says debt is cheap leverage and any business that is not using it is leaving growth on the table.

I think of debt as a tool. A good one, used for the right job. The thing that separates it from every other source of capital is that it has a due date, and the due date does not care how the year went.

## What debt is good for

Debt works best when it funds something that produces cash on a predictable schedule, ideally faster than the loan has to be repaid.

**Equipment and assets that pay for themselves.** A piece of equipment that increases capacity or lowers costs can generate the cash to service the loan that bought it.

**Working capital for a business with reliable receivables.** Labor businesses know this one well. Payroll goes out every week, and clients pay on their own schedule. A line of credit that bridges that gap, for a business with creditworthy customers, is one of the most useful tools available.

**Acquisitions with stable cash flow.** Buying an established business with proven earnings can be financed in part by those earnings, if the price and terms are reasonable.

**Real estate the business would otherwise rent.** In the right circumstances, owning the building can be cheaper over time than leasing it.

Programs like the [SBA 7(a) loan](https://www.sba.gov/loans/7a-loans/) exist for many of these purposes, including working capital, equipment, real estate, refinancing, and changes of ownership. Whatever the source, the logic is the same: the thing being financed should be able to carry the payment.

## What debt is bad for

Debt is a poor fit for anything where the timing of the return is uncertain.

**Unproven ventures.** A new product that may or may not find customers is exactly the kind of thing that should be funded with equity or retained earnings, not borrowed money. If the venture takes longer than expected, which most do, the payments keep coming anyway.

**Covering losses.** Borrowing to cover an operating shortfall without a clear plan to fix the underlying problem usually makes the eventual problem larger.

**Growth for its own sake.** Expansion funded by debt magnifies both outcomes. If the expansion works, the return on the owner's capital is higher. If it does not, the business carries the cost of a failed bet along with the debt that financed it.

This is one reason we prefer to fund new ventures in stages, with cash the operating businesses produce, rather than borrowing against an idea. I wrote about that approach in [Let the boring business fund the interesting one](https://t47group.com/blog/cash-flow-businesses-fund-moonshots/).

## Questions to answer before borrowing

Before signing anything, I want clear answers to a few questions.

1. **What exactly will this money do, and how will it produce the cash to repay it?** If the answer is vague, the loan is probably premature.
2. **What happens in a bad year?** Run the payments against a weaker version of the business, not the plan. If the business cannot carry the payment in a bad year, the loan is too large.
3. **What are the covenants?** Many loans come with conditions about financial ratios, reporting, or other debt. Breaking one can create problems even when every payment is on time.
4. **Who is guaranteeing it?** Many small business loans require personal guarantees from owners. Understand exactly what you are putting at risk.
5. **Is the rate fixed or variable?** A variable rate that looks reasonable today can look very different in a few years.
6. **Can it be prepaid?** Flexibility to pay down debt early, without penalty, is worth something.

These are general questions, not legal or financial advice. A good banker, accountant, and attorney should review the actual terms.

## Debt as a benchmark

Even when a business has no debt, it is a useful benchmark. Paying down a loan produces a guaranteed return equal to its interest rate. Any other use of cash has to beat that, after accounting for risk.

That comparison clarifies a lot of decisions. A project that cannot clearly outperform simply paying down debt is probably not worth the risk. I described how we use that comparison in [Where the next dollar goes](https://t47group.com/blog/where-the-next-dollar-goes/).

## Respect the due date

Most businesses that get into trouble with debt are not reckless. They borrow for reasonable purposes, on reasonable terms, based on plans that turn out to be too optimistic.

The protection is not avoiding debt altogether. It is borrowing only for things that can reliably carry the payment, sizing the loan to survive a bad year, and keeping enough reserve that a slow quarter does not become a default.

Used that way, debt is one of the most useful tools an owner has. Used any other way, it turns a manageable mistake into a much larger one.
