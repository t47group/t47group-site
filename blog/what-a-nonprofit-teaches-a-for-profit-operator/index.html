What running a nonprofit teaches a for-profit operator

Nino Mihilli· November 2, 2026 ·3 min read

Most of my working life has been spent in businesses that answer to customers and to the bottom line. NMWWD, the nonprofit focused on wrong-way driving, answers to something different. There is no product to sell and no margin to protect. There is a problem that hurts people, and a question of whether we are doing anything useful about it.

Running it has taught me things I did not expect, and many of them have changed how I think about the companies.

Mission has to be specific to be useful

In business, it is easy to write a mission statement that sounds good and means very little. Customers do not usually hold you to it.

A nonprofit lives or dies by how clearly it can say what it is trying to change. Wrong-way driving is a specific problem. That specificity helps in every conversation, whether with volunteers, partners, or people who have been affected. Everyone knows what we are talking about and why it matters.

It made me look harder at how clearly our companies state what they exist to do. A team that can describe its purpose in one plain sentence makes better decisions than one working from a paragraph of aspirations.

Volunteers cannot be managed like employees

In a company, people are paid to show up, and that creates a baseline of commitment. In a nonprofit, much of the work depends on people giving their time freely.

That changes leadership entirely. You cannot rely on authority. You have to rely on clarity, respect for people's time, and a constant connection to why the work matters. Volunteers who feel their time is wasted on unclear tasks or pointless meetings stop coming.

The lesson for business is uncomfortable. Many of the habits that drive volunteers away also wear down paid employees. They just take longer to leave, and they often stay and disengage instead. Treating everyone's time as if they were volunteering it would improve most companies.

Every dollar is visible

Nonprofits operate under a level of scrutiny about spending that most private businesses never face. Donors and partners want to know where the money went and what it accomplished.

That pressure is healthy. It forces you to tie every expense to an outcome and to be able to explain the connection. Applying the same standard to a for-profit company, asking of each expense what it is supposed to produce and whether it did, catches waste that would otherwise go unnoticed for years.

Partnerships do most of the work

A small nonprofit cannot solve a large problem alone. Road safety involves transportation agencies, law enforcement, engineers, educators, and families. Most of the progress comes from getting the right people talking to each other and supporting work that others are already doing.

That made me more aware of how much businesses try to do everything themselves. Often the better move is to find who is already good at the thing you need and build a real partnership with them. It is slower to set up and usually stronger over time.

Outcomes are harder to measure, and that matters

In business, the scoreboard is clear. Revenue, margin, cash. In a nonprofit focused on prevention, success is often something that did not happen, and that is hard to count.

Working with that uncertainty has made me more humble about metrics in general. Some of the most important things a company does, like building trust, developing people, and avoiding a bad decision, never show up neatly in a report. The numbers matter. They are not the whole picture, and a leader who forgets that will tend to underinvest in everything that cannot be measured.

Why it belongs alongside the companies

People sometimes see the nonprofit as separate from the business work. I see it as part of the same life. It reflects the same faith and the same belief that what we build should leave things better than we found them.

It is also, quietly, one of the better leadership schools I have attended. A cause that depends on goodwill teaches you to lead without leverage, spend without waste, and keep going when results are slow to appear. Every business could use more of that.
