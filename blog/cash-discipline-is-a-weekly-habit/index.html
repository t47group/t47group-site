Cash discipline is a weekly habit, not a year-end event

Nino Mihilli· October 14, 2026 ·4 min read

Most businesses that get into trouble were not unprofitable. They ran out of cash while profitable, or while profit was about to arrive, which is a different problem with the same ending.

Profit is an accounting idea. Cash is what pays people on Friday. In a staffing business that difference is impossible to ignore, because payroll goes out every week and clients pay on their own schedule. That gap teaches you, quickly, that cash needs its own attention separate from the income statement.

The good news is that cash discipline is not complicated. It is mostly a handful of habits, done every week, whether or not anything seems wrong.

Look at the cash every week

Once a week I want a short view of where cash stands and where it is going over the next thirteen weeks. Money in by expected date. Money out by due date. The resulting balance, week by week.

It does not need to be precise. It needs to be current. A rough thirteen-week view updated every Monday is far more useful than a detailed forecast built once a quarter, because the useful information is in the change from last week, not in the number itself.

When the projected low point moves, I want to know why before it becomes urgent.

Treat receivables as a sales problem

Slow-paying customers are usually discussed as an accounting issue. They are a relationship issue, and they belong partly to whoever owns the relationship.

Clear terms at the start help. So does invoicing promptly and correctly, which sounds obvious and is frequently where the delay begins. After that, a regular, polite follow-up rhythm matters more than any collections policy. Most customers who pay late are not refusing. They are prioritizing whoever asked most recently.

And sometimes the right answer is that a customer who consistently pays late is a more expensive customer than the invoice suggests. That should show up in how you price them, or whether you keep them.

Know your fixed commitments

Payroll, rent, debt service, insurance, the software and vendor contracts that renew automatically. These are the obligations that do not flex when revenue does.

I like to see them listed in one place with their dates. It makes it obvious which weeks are heavy, and it makes it easier to say no to new fixed commitments that would quietly raise the floor. Variable costs can be adjusted. Fixed ones usually cannot, at least not quickly.

Hold a reserve and leave it alone

Every operating company should have a cash reserve sized to its own risk. A business with steady monthly billing needs less. One with long payment cycles, seasonal swings, or a few large customers needs more.

The discipline is not in setting the number. It is in not borrowing from it for things that are not emergencies. A reserve that gets used for an opportunity that seemed too good to pass up is no longer a reserve.

Separate growth spending from operating spending

Growth almost always consumes cash before it produces any. New hires need to be paid before they are productive. New clients need to be served before they pay. New products need to be built before they sell.

That is fine, as long as it is planned and funded on purpose. The trouble starts when growth spending is mixed in with normal operations, and nobody notices that the business is financing its own expansion out of the payroll account.

I want growth investments to have their own line, their own expected timeline, and a point at which we check whether they are working.

Keep a short list of levers

When the thirteen-week view shows a problem coming, I want to already know what we would do about it. Which payments can be timed differently. Which spending can pause. Which customers might pay early if asked. Whether a line of credit is in place before it is needed, rather than requested in the middle of a squeeze.

Deciding these things calmly, ahead of time, is what turns a cash problem into a scheduling problem.

Why it is a habit and not a project

Cash discipline does not reward intensity. It rewards consistency. A business that looks at its cash for twenty minutes every week will almost always be in better shape than one that does a deep review twice a year.

It is also one of the few habits that transfers cleanly across very different companies. The same weekly routine works for a staffing business, a software studio, and a consumer app, because the underlying question never changes: what do we have, what is coming, and what is going out.
