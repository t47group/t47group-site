Stewardship is a better frame than ownership

Nino Mihilli· October 21, 2026 ·3 min read

On paper, I own companies. In practice, I have come to think of myself as a steward of them, and the difference has shaped more of my decisions than any management framework I have read.

Ownership says: this is mine, and I can do what I want with it. Stewardship says: this has been entrusted to me, and I will be asked how I handled it.

For someone who takes faith seriously, the second view is not a metaphor. It is how I understand what I have been given. But I think the frame is useful whether or not you share that belief, because it changes the questions you ask.

Capital is held in trust

When you think of capital as yours, it is easy to treat it as a scoreboard. More is better, and risk is a game you are entitled to play.

When you think of it as entrusted, the questions change. Is this money doing something useful? Am I putting it at risk in proportion to what it could accomplish? Would I be comfortable explaining this decision to someone who cares about how I used what I was given?

That does not make me averse to risk. Building anything involves risk, and burying what you have been given out of fear is its own failure. It does make me more careful about the kind of risk. Calculated bets on things I understand are good stewardship. Gambling on things I do not understand, because the upside looks exciting, usually is not.

People are not resources

Business language calls employees human resources. I understand the reason for the term, and I do not think it is malicious. But it encourages a way of thinking where people are inputs, interchangeable and measured mostly by what they produce.

A steward sees it differently. The people in a company have been placed, for a time, under your leadership. Their livelihoods depend in part on your decisions. Their working lives are shaped by the environment you create.

That changes how I think about layoffs, about pay, about how much notice someone gets, about whether a manager is allowed to treat people badly as long as the numbers are good. The rule I keep returning to is Luke 6:31: treat others the way I would want to be treated in their place. It is not complicated. It is just inconvenient often enough to matter.

Long horizons come naturally

Stewardship assumes you will hand something on. That might be to a successor, to the next generation, or simply to the version of the business that exists in ten years.

That assumption pushes toward decisions that compound. Investing in people who will grow. Building systems that outlast any one person. Maintaining relationships that will matter long after the current deal is finished. It pushes away from decisions that maximize this year at the expense of the next five.

This is part of why we tend to hold companies rather than sell them. A steward who plans to stay thinks differently from an owner who plans to exit.

Prayer and diligence work together

People sometimes ask whether faith means I pray about decisions instead of analyzing them. The answer is that I do both, and I do not see them as competing.

Prayer, for me, is about clarity of motive. Am I making this decision for the right reasons? Is there pride or fear driving it that I have not admitted? Is there someone affected that I have not considered?

Analysis is about the facts. What do the numbers say? What are the risks? What has to be true for this to work?

Skipping either one is a mistake. Analysis without examining your motives tends to produce clever decisions for bad reasons. Prayer without analysis is not faith. It is avoiding the work you were given to do.

Accountability is the point

The core of stewardship is that you will be asked how you handled what you were given. That is a sobering thought, and a useful one.

It means the measure is not only whether the business grew. It is whether it was run with integrity, whether people were treated well, whether the resources went toward something worthwhile. Those are harder standards than a profit target, and I fall short of them regularly.

But they are the right standards. And holding them, imperfectly, has made me a better operator than I would have been otherwise. More on how that plays out day to day lives on my personal site.
