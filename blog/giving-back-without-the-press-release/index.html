Giving back without the press release

Nino Mihilli· October 19, 2026 ·3 min read

A lot of corporate giving is designed to be seen. A large check, a photo, a post. I understand why. Visibility can help a cause, and there is nothing wrong with a business being associated with something good.

But I have found that the giving that matters most in a small company is usually less visible. It is built into how the business operates, it happens on a schedule, and most of the people who benefit never see a logo.

Start with what the business is good at

Every company has something it can offer that is worth more than money. A staffing business knows how to organize people and get them where they need to be. A software studio can build a simple tool that saves a nonprofit hours every week. A company with a warehouse has space, equipment, and people who know how to move things.

Money is useful and often needed. But the contributions that last tend to come from matching what the business already does well with what an organization lacks. That kind of help is harder to replace and more likely to continue.

Put it on the calendar

Good intentions without a schedule rarely survive a busy quarter. If giving back depends on someone finding time, it will not happen in the months when it is needed most.

What works is treating it like any other commitment. A set number of volunteer days. A recurring partnership with an organization you know well. A budget line, even a modest one, that is decided at the start of the year rather than at the end, when it competes with everything else.

The holidays are a natural focus, and we do a lot around them. But many organizations are flooded with offers in December and struggle for help in March. Spreading involvement across the year is often more useful than concentrating it.

Let the team lead some of it

Some of the best ideas for where to give come from employees, not owners. People know which local schools, churches, food banks, and community groups are doing good work, because they live near them and their families rely on them.

Giving the team a voice in where the company directs its time and resources does two things. It points that effort toward places with real need, and it makes the involvement belong to the whole company rather than to the founder's personal interests.

Be careful with the marketing

There is a fine line between telling people what you care about and using a cause to sell. I try to stay on the right side of it.

My general rule is that the organization should benefit more from the publicity than we do. If a post would help them recruit volunteers or raise money, it is worth doing. If it mostly makes us look good, it is probably better left unsaid. Customers and employees can tell the difference, and the companies that confuse the two tend to lose credibility on both fronts.

Connect it to something personal

The causes people stick with are usually ones they have a reason to care about. For me, that includes my faith and the communities around the church, and it includes road safety, which led to the wrong-way driving nonprofit I started.

I do not think every business owner needs to start a foundation. But I do think it helps to know why you care about what you support. That reason is what keeps you involved in the years when the business is under pressure and it would be easy to let the commitment slide.

It makes the company better

Giving back is not a business strategy, and I am wary of anyone who frames it as one. You do it because it is right.

That said, it is hard to ignore the effects. Teams that volunteer together tend to work together better. Employees who see their company show up for the community take a different kind of pride in where they work. And a business that is known locally for doing the right thing quietly finds that people are more willing to trust it.

Those are side effects, not the reason. But they are real, and they compound over years.

The quiet version lasts longer

A single large gesture is easy to announce and easy to forget. A steady commitment, carried out every year by people who care about it, becomes part of what the company is.

That is the version I want. Not a press release, but a habit.
